Why so many optimizers

Gradient descent has exactly two bad habits. Momentum fixes the first, adaptive learning rates fix the second, and Adam is the one that does both. That is the entire family tree.

Your deck ends on slide 128 with: “Adam is a much more efficient way of searching for these minimums, so you will see us use it for our code!” — and then shows a graph of five algorithms beating each other, unexplained.

Here is the explanation. Start from plain gradient descent, which the deck describes correctly on slides 118–125: measure the slope, take a step downhill, repeat.

θ ← θ − η ∇C(θ)        # eta is the learning rate

This rule has two specific, nameable weaknesses. Every optimizer you have heard of is a patch for one of them.

1Problem one: it forgets everything it just did

Each step is computed from the current gradient alone. The optimizer has no memory, so it cannot tell the difference between “the ground keeps sloping this way, I should build up speed” and “I am bouncing between two walls and getting nowhere.”

In practice loss surfaces are full of ravines: directions that are very steep across and very shallow along. Plain gradient descent takes big useless steps across the ravine and tiny useful ones along it.

Momentum — the fix

Keep a running average of recent gradients and step in that direction instead. Consistent directions accumulate; directions that keep flipping sign cancel themselves out. The usual picture is a ball rolling downhill rather than a hiker recomputing the slope at every footstep.

2Problem two: one learning rate for every parameter

η is a single number applied to all several-million weights. But a weight receiving huge gradients and a weight receiving tiny ones need completely different step sizes. Pick η small enough to be safe for the steep parameters and the flat ones will barely move in your lifetime; pick it large enough for the flat ones and the steep ones explode.

Adaptive rates — the fix

Give every parameter its own step size, derived from how large its own gradients have recently been. Divide each update by the root-mean-square of that parameter's recent gradients: big-gradient parameters get damped, small-gradient parameters get amplified. This is RMSProp. (Its predecessor AdaGrad summed gradients from the beginning of time, so its step sizes shrank monotonically to zero and training stalled. RMSProp replaced the running sum with a decaying average, which fixed it.)[1]

3Adam = both fixes in one

Momentum tracks the mean of recent gradients. RMSProp tracks the mean square. Adam tracks both and divides one by the other — hence the official description, “based on adaptive estimation of first-order and second-order moments.”[2]

4Race them

Below is a ravine — a bowl twelve times steeper across than along. All four optimizers start at the same point with the same learning rate, and the update rules are the real published ones. Press Run.

What to look for:

  1. SGD (red) zig-zags across the ravine while creeping along it. Every one of those zig-zags is wasted work.
  2. Momentum (amber) zig-zags too — but the side-to-side components keep cancelling while the along-the-floor component accumulates. It overshoots, then comes back.
  3. RMSProp (green) barely zig-zags at all. The steep direction gets big gradients, so it divides by a big number and takes small steps there.
  4. Adam (blue) combines the two — damped across, fast along.

Now the part worth ten minutes of your attention: drag the learning rate up and watch them fail in order.

Learning rateWhat happens
0.17SGD diverges. Its step across the ravine now overshoots to a point further from the floor than it started, and each bounce is bigger than the last.
0.32Momentum diverges too. Momentum bought roughly a factor of two in tolerance — helpful, but it is still applying one raw step size to a direction that cannot take it.
0.40 (slider max)RMSProp and Adam are still going. Neither breaks anywhere in the slider's range.

That ordering is the whole argument for adaptive optimizers, in one experiment.

The real reason everyone uses Adam

Not that it converges fastest — often it does not; watch RMSProp's loss hover while SGD's plummets once SGD is in the valley. It is that Adam works without you tuning it. On its very first step m̂/√v̂ is exactly ±1, so Adam moves by exactly the learning rate in every direction — here, ±0.08 along both axes, even though the two gradients are −2.55 and +12.6. The learning rate stops meaning “how far per unit of gradient” and starts meaning “how far per step”. That is what makes a badly guessed value survivable, and in practice that matters far more than winning a race.

5What to actually do

from tensorflow import keras

# 99% of the time, start here.
model.compile(optimizer='adam', loss='mse')

# Same thing, written out, so you can see the knob that matters.
model.compile(
    optimizer=keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.001),  # <- the default
    loss='mse'
)
  • Default to Adam at lr=0.001. Change the learning rate before you change the optimizer — it is by far the higher-leverage knob.
  • Loss is exploding or producing NaN: learning rate too high. Divide by 10.
  • Loss is falling but glacially: learning rate too low. Multiply by 3.
  • Loss drops fast then flattens above where you want it: you need to decay the rate as you go — which is exactly what ReduceLROnPlateau does, in Lesson 4.
  • You need the last fraction of a percent and have time to tune: SGD(momentum=0.9) with a hand-built schedule still beats Adam on some benchmarks.
Worth knowing — the deck stops in 2015

The deck cites Kingma & Ba (2015) and stops. Since then the default has quietly moved to AdamW, which fixes how Adam interacts with weight decay (the L2 regularization you will meet in Lesson 4). Plain Adam lets the adaptive scaling eat the decay term; AdamW applies it separately. Use keras.optimizers.AdamW() when you are also regularizing.
