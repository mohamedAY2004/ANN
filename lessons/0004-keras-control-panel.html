Lesson 04 Artificial Neural Networks Cheat sheet

The Keras control panel

Your deck stops at backpropagation. Everything that makes a model actually work in practice lives in the two hundred lines after that — and it is all aimed at one enemy.

Lesson 1 left you with an uncomfortable fact: a network with enough hidden units can fit any curve. Which means it can fit the curve through your training data including the noise — and noise, by definition, does not repeat on new data.

The one idea

The goal was never to fit the training data. The goal is to perform on data the model has never seen. Everything below is a way of saying: do not trust the training loss. Keras gives you two kinds of tool for this — regularizers, which change the model so it prefers simpler explanations, and callbacks, which watch training and intervene while it runs.

1See it memorise

Below: 32 hidden units, 14 noisy training points, trained live. Press Train with all three tools off and watch the two loss curves separate.

The blue training loss falls forever — of course it does, the model is being rewarded for exactly that. The red validation loss falls, bottoms out, and then climbs. After that turning point every further epoch is making the model worse at its actual job, and the training loss never once tells you.

Look at the left panel too. The network stops drawing the smooth dashed truth and starts detouring to pass exactly through each dot. That detour is the overfitting.

Now switch the tools on, one at a time, retraining each time:

ToolWhat it does to the pictureMechanism
L2 penalty Curve gets visibly calmer. Final validation loss drops by about a third, and the two curves stay close instead of diverging. Adds the sum of squared weights to the loss. Big weights now cost something, and only sharp wiggles need big weights.
Dropout Training loss jumps sharply and goes noisy. The wiggle disappears — but validation barely improves. Read the note below. Randomly switches off 15% of hidden units each step. No unit can rely on any other, so the network cannot build a fragile private conspiracy to nail one data point.
Early stopping Training halts at the red line and weights roll back to “best”. Same benefit as L2, for no thought at all. Watches validation loss, waits patience epochs for an improvement, gives up, and restores the best weights it saw.
An honest result, and a real lesson

Dropout is the weakest of the three on this particular problem, and it is worth understanding why rather than pretending otherwise. Fourteen data points and 32 units is a tiny problem; switching off a sixth of the network on every step is such a strong constraint that the model swings from memorising to underfitting. You can watch it happen: the training loss does not just get slightly worse, it jumps by two orders of magnitude.

Dropout earns its reputation on large networks with plenty of data, where there is redundancy to exploit. On a small problem, L2 and early stopping are the better tools. Regularizers are not interchangeable, and more regularization is not better regularization.

Note the detail that generalises, though: every regularizer makes the training loss worse. That is the trade being made. If switching one on does not degrade your training loss at all, it is not doing anything.

2Regularizers — set at build time

from tensorflow import keras
from tensorflow.keras import layers, regularizers

model = keras.Sequential([
    layers.Dense(128, activation='relu',
                 kernel_regularizer=regularizers.L2(1e-4)),   # penalise big weights
    layers.Dropout(0.3),                                  # drop 30% of units per step
    layers.Dense(64, activation='relu'),
    layers.Dropout(0.3),
    layers.Dense(1)                                       # regression output, no activation
])

Two things beginners get wrong here:

  • Dropout is a layer, not an argument. It sits after the layer it is thinning. It is automatically inactive at prediction time — Keras handles that for you, so never try to turn it off by hand.
  • kernel_regularizer penalises the weights. That is the one you want. bias_regularizer is almost never useful (biases do not cause wiggle), and activity_regularizer penalises the layer's outputs, which is a different and rarer intent.[1]
The quiet third regularizer

Everything in Lesson 2 and 3 also bears on this. A smaller network is the most direct regularizer of all — fewer hinges, less capacity to memorise. Reach for it before you reach for dropout. The deck's habit of stacking large Dense layers by default is a habit worth questioning.

3Callbacks — set at fit time

A callback is an object Keras calls at fixed moments during training: start of epoch, end of batch, end of epoch. That hook is enough to build all of the following.

callbacks = [
    # 1. Stop when validation loss stops improving, and rewind.
    keras.callbacks.EarlyStopping(
        monitor='val_loss',
        patience=10,                  # epochs of no improvement to tolerate
        restore_best_weights=True    # <- without this you keep the WORST weights
    ),

    # 2. Keep the best model on disk, whatever happens afterwards.
    keras.callbacks.ModelCheckpoint(
        'best.keras', monitor='val_loss', save_best_only=True
    ),

    # 3. When progress stalls, take smaller steps instead of giving up.
    keras.callbacks.ReduceLROnPlateau(
        monitor='val_loss', factor=0.5, patience=5, min_lr=1e-6
    ),

    # 4. Log everything for TensorBoard (deck slide 5 promises this).
    keras.callbacks.TensorBoard(log_dir='./logs')
]

model.fit(X_train, y_train,
          validation_data=(X_val, y_val),   # required - callbacks watch val_loss
          epochs=500,                        # set it high; EarlyStopping decides the real end
          callbacks=callbacks)
The one that bites everyone

restore_best_weights defaults to False.[2] Leave it off and EarlyStopping waits patience epochs of the model getting worse, then stops and keeps those degraded weights. You get the worst model of the last ten epochs. Always pass restore_best_weights=True.

Two consequences worth internalising:

  • With EarlyStopping attached, epochs stops being a hyperparameter you tune. Set it absurdly high and let the callback find the end. That is one fewer thing to guess.
  • ReduceLROnPlateau is the direct answer to Lesson 3's last symptom — loss falls fast, then flattens too high. Big steps got you into the valley; smaller steps get you to the bottom of it.
