Lesson 02 Artificial Neural Networks Cheat sheet

Choosing the bend

Your deck lists sigmoid, tanh, ReLU and softmax as one menu. They are not one menu. They answer two completely separate questions, and confusing them is the most common way beginners build a broken model.

Lesson 1 established that you need a non-linearity. This lesson is about which. And the first move is to notice that “which activation?” is really two questions wearing one coat:

The one idea

Hidden layers and the output layer choose activations for opposite reasons. A hidden layer picks one that lets the learning signal travel back through it — that is an engineering choice, and the answer is almost always ReLU. The output layer picks one that gives the answer the right shape — that is decided entirely by your task, and you get no freedom at all.

1Hidden layers: the gradient has to get home

Training works by measuring the error at the output and passing blame backwards, layer by layer, all the way to the first weight. That is backpropagation, and mechanically it is a long chain of multiplications — the deck writes it on slide 160 as δˡ = (wˡ⁺¹)ᵀ δˡ⁺¹ ⊙ σ′(zˡ).

Ignore the notation and read the shape of it. At every single layer, the blame signal gets multiplied by σ′ — the slope of that layer's activation function. Ten layers means ten multiplications.

So the question “is this a good hidden activation?” becomes a very concrete one: what happens to a number multiplied by this function's slope, ten times in a row?

2Watch a gradient vanish

Left plot: the function. Right plot: its slope — the thing that actually gets multiplied on the way back. Start on Sigmoid and drag the depth slider.

The number to sit with: sigmoid's slope never exceeds 0.25, at exactly one point, x = 0. Everywhere else it is smaller. So in the best possible case, ten sigmoid layers scale the gradient by 0.2510 ≈ 0.00000095.

The first layer of that network is being told to adjust itself by roughly one-millionth of what the last layer is told. It is, for practical purposes, frozen. This is the vanishing gradient problem — the reason deep networks were considered untrainable for most of the 1990s and 2000s, and the single sentence hiding behind your deck's throwaway line on slide 63 that “ReLu has been found to have very good performance, especially when dealing with the issue of vanishing gradient.”

Now click ReLU. Its slope on the positive side is exactly 1. Multiply by 1 as many times as you like; nothing shrinks. That is the whole trick.

ReLU's one failure mode

Look at the left half of ReLU's slope plot: it is zero. A unit whose input has drifted negative for every training example receives exactly zero gradient, so it can never update, so it stays negative forever. This is a dead unit, and a large learning rate can kill off a big fraction of a layer in one bad step.[1] Leaky ReLU exists precisely for this: tilt the flat half slightly, and a dead unit keeps a thin thread back to life.

3The output layer: no freedom, just matching

Hidden layers are an engineering judgement. The output layer is not a judgement at all — it is determined by the question you are asking. And it always travels with a matching loss function, because the two are designed as a pair.

Your taskOutput layerLossWhy
Regression
predict a house price		 Dense(1)
no activation		 mse The answer is an unbounded number. Squashing it into 0–1 would be sabotage.
Binary
spam or not spam		 Dense(1, 'sigmoid') binary_crossentropy One number in 0–1, read as P(yes).
Multi-class, exclusive
which one digit is this		 Dense(10, 'softmax') categorical_crossentropy Ten numbers that sum to 1. Raising one must lower the others.
Multi-label, non-exclusive
tag a photo: beach, family, sunset		 Dense(K, 'sigmoid') binary_crossentropy K independent yes/no calls. They must not compete.

Rows three and four are where people come unstuck, and they are exactly the distinction your deck draws on slides 68–91 between mutually exclusive and non-exclusive classes. The mechanism behind it is simple:

# softmax: the denominator couples every output together
softmax(z)ᵢ = e^zᵢ / Σⱼ e^zⱼ      # -> [0.1, 0.6, 0.3]  sums to 1

# sigmoid: each output is computed alone, unaware of the others
sigmoid(z)ᵢ = 1 / (1 + e^-zᵢ)     # -> [0.8, 0.2, 0.3]  sums to anything

Use softmax on a photo-tagging model and you have told it that a photo tagged beach is therefore less likely to be family — which is nonsense, and it will dutifully learn the nonsense.

Practical note the deck omits

In Keras you will often see the output layer left raw (no activation) with from_logits=True passed to the loss instead. Same model, better numerical stability — the exponential and the logarithm get to cancel inside one function rather than fighting each other across two.[2]

4What to actually do

  • Hidden layers: start with relu. Always. It is the default for good reason and you need a specific complaint before you change it.
  • Loss stuck and a lot of your units output zero: suspect dead units. Lower the learning rate first; try LeakyReLU second.
  • Output layer: read it off the table above. There is no judgement call here.
  • Sigmoid and tanh in hidden layers: effectively retired for feed-forward networks. You will still meet them inside LSTM and GRU cells, where saturation is being used deliberately as a gate rather than suffered as a side effect.
Read this next — primary source

Stanford CS231n, “Commonly used activation functions”. Two pages, written by people who train these things for a living, and the source for the dying-ReLU advice above. Their closing recommendation is blunt and worth internalising: use ReLU, watch your learning rates, and try Leaky ReLU if you see dead units.
